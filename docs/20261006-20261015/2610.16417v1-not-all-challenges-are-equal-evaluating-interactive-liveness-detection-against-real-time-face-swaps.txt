Abstract: Remote identity verification assumes that a camera stream shows a live person, yet public
real-time face-swap tools can replace that person before the stream reaches the verifier. Passive deepfake
detectors generalize poorly to new generators, while interactive challenges are often selected without
evidence of which gestures expose current attacks. We evaluate randomized gesture challenges in 108
live sessions with six subjects, 24 impersonated identities, and two face-swap tools, DeepFaceLive and
FaceFusion, injected through a virtual camera. The static calibration segment reaches a session-level
AUC of 0.878 with a frozen UCF spatial detector, while the mean across the active-gesture windows
reaches 0.988. Pose and combined gestures have the highest point estimates, whereas expressions show
no clear improvement. Hand occlusion against FaceFusion, configured with an occlusion mask, scores
below the static baseline. The end-to-end time required to satisfy the validator also distinguishes
attacks from genuine sessions, achieving a session-level AUC of 0.732, although this signal includes
synthesis and transport delays. These results show that challenge sets should be tested against current
attack configurations and that response time can provide an additional, deployment-specific signal.
Keywords: Deepfake, Face Swapping, Liveness Detection, Interactive Verification, Passive Detection,
Remote Identity Verification, Biometric Security, Presentation Attack Detection

Introduction
Remote identity verification, from video calls to online banking onboarding, rests on a
strong premise: that the camera shows a real person, in real time, in front of a real lens. Deep
learning has reduced the cost of a convincing synthetic face to minutes of setup [FMH23].
A single public photo feeds a real-time face-swap pipeline on a consumer graphics card,
and the attacker routes the synthesized stream into the call through a virtual camera driver.
No server compromise, network tampering, or physical artifact is needed. The threat is real:
a widely reported case includes a 25 million USD transfer authorized after a video call with
a deepfake CFO [CN24].
Traditional passive detection searches individual frames for digital inconsistencies such as
blending seams or unnatural textures. It catches older generators but generalizes poorly
across the moving target of new tools [Pe24], and deployed liveness systems fall to injected

A natural response is to add an interactive layer to the passive pipeline. The user performs a
short, randomized sequence of actions, such as rotating the head, changing expression, or
covering part of the face, within a fixed time. These actions can expose synthesis errors
when the swap must reproduce unusual poses or occlusions in real time. Randomization
reduces the risk of simple pre-recorded replay, although it does not prevent an attacker from
generating and injecting a live response. Such challenges are widely deployed in commercial
liveness verification [Li22; WLL24], but their selection is often based on intuition. This
proof-of-concept study measures which gesture categories improve detection relative to
current real-time face swaps and identifies cases in which a challenge provides little or no
benefit.
We implement a hybrid architecture that combines frozen passive detectors with randomized
gesture challenges and evaluate it in 108 live sessions with six subjects, 24 impersonated
identities, and two real-time face-swap tools, DeepFaceLive [Li23] and FaceFusion [Ru23],
injected through a virtual camera. Using the raw UCF [Ya23] spatial score without fusion
weights, the static calibration segment reaches a session-level AUC of 0.878, while the mean
across the active-gesture windows reaches 0.988. The improvement is not uniform: pose
and combined gestures have the highest point estimates, expressions show no clear gain,
and occlusion against FaceFusion scores below the static baseline. Because FaceFusion
was tested only with its occlusion mask enabled, this result is consistent with a tool-specific
countermeasure but does not isolate its causal effect. The campaign also revealed that the
tested attack pipelines take longer to satisfy the validator. This end-to-end timing signal
reaches a session-level AUC of 0.732, but it includes synthesis and virtual-camera delay
as well as user response time. The work described in this paper results from a previously
completed master‘s thesis [Dr26].
In summary, the main outcomes of this work are:
•
An end-to-end liveness pipeline combining frozen passive detection with randomized
gesture challenges, evaluated in 108 live genuine and attack sessions.
•
A subject-clustered comparison of gesture categories against a static baseline, showing
aggregate improvement but different effects across attack configurations.
•
An empirical evaluation of verifier-observed time-to-validation as a complementary
end-to-end signal during live face-swap attacks.

Background and Related Work
Passive detection and its generalization gap. Passive detection analyzes video without
requiring additional user action; its cues range from synthesis artifacts to physiological incon-

sistencies, such as the spatial and temporal biological signals used by FakeCatcher [CDY20].
Artifact-based detectors can learn cues associated with the manipulations present in their
training data, and their performance can decline when tested on different manipulation
methods or data distributions. Cross-dataset evaluations and an in-the-wild benchmark of
deepfakes circulated in 2024 document this generalization gap [Ch25; Pe24]. Methods based
on self-blended training images [SY22], common forgery features [Ya23], and pretrained
vision-language features [OLL23] aim to improve transfer beyond the manipulations used for
training. The deployed pipeline uses UCF [Ya23] and CViT2 [Wo24] with frozen pretrained
weights, while the evaluation below reports raw UCF scores on live DeepFaceLive and
FaceFusion outputs without generator-specific adaptation.
Real-time face-swap tools. Mature open-source toolchains make live face swapping acces-
sible. DeepFaceLab [Li23] trains a model for a specific identity pair, while DeepFaceLive
applies the exported .dfm model to a webcam stream. Community models for public
figures can be downloaded without training a new model. FaceFusion [Ru23] instead swaps
identities from reference images using InsightFace embeddings [Gu18]. Its optional XSeg
occlusion mask preserves foreground objects, such as hands, by excluding them from the
swapped face region. The attacker can route either tool through a virtual camera into an
application that reads the operating-system camera API [WLL24]. Residual artifacts include
flicker, mask-boundary errors, identity drift at extreme poses, and failures around occluders;
interactive challenges are intended to expose these weaknesses.
Interactive liveness detection. Interactive liveness asks the user to perform an action within
a time window and verifies the action in the video. Common examples include head rotation,
blinking measured through the Eye Aspect Ratio [SČ16], expressions measured through
landmarks or dedicated networks [Mi18], and hand-over-face occlusion. A pre-rendered
deepfake cannot answer an arbitrary instruction, but a real-time swap can transfer the motion
of its human driver [Li22]. Motion-only checks are therefore insufficient, as demonstrated
against commercial services [Li22] and mobile SDKs [WLL24]. Hybrid designs instead use
the challenge to provoke synthesis artifacts that a passive detector can measure [ŠFM24].
DF-Captcha proposed this challenge-response principle [Mi22], while GOTCHA evaluated
visible degradation on recordings from 47 users and 2022-era pipelines [MHM24]. We extend
this evidence with live end-to-end sessions, subject-clustered estimates of category-level
effects against current attack configurations, and an empirical analysis of verifier-observed
time-to-validation.

Proposed Verification Pipeline
The pipeline is a thin-client design: the browser only captures the webcam stream and
displays instruction overlays, while all analysis runs on the server. A central controller
manages the session as a state machine, drawing random actions from a challenge generator
and enforcing a per-action timeout. Two branches analyze the incoming frames in parallel:
a passive branch scoring every frame for deepfake artifacts and an interactive branch

Pre-processing. Frames are processed at approximately 15 FPS through the InsightFace
buffalo_l pack [Gu18] (face detection, landmark regression, ArcFace head [De19]).
Landmarks are stabilized with a One Euro filter [CRV12] and an affine alignment produces
a canonical crop per detector. The pipeline runs with a P95 per-frame latency below 40 ms
on a single consumer GPU.
Passive branch. Two detectors with frozen public weights score each aligned crop:
UCF [Ya23] produces a spatial score per frame, while CViT2 [Wo24] predictions are
averaged over sliding frame windows. The models were not trained on outputs from
DeepFaceLive or FaceFusion. An additional identity tracker anchors an ArcFace embedding
on the first valid frame and monitors cosine similarity throughout the session. The deployed
verdict uses these signals, but the gesture comparisons reported below use only the raw
UCF spatial score.
Interactive branch. Action validation runs on the MediaPipe Face Mesh and Hands
models [Lu19] (468 face landmarks with blendshapes, 21 per hand). The gesture pool covers
four categories: pose (head rotations and leans, verified through Euler angles), expression
(smile, open mouth, eyebrows, verified through landmark distances and blendshapes),
occlusion (covering an eye, the nose, or the mouth, verified by intersecting a hand mask [Sá23]
with the facial region), and complex (two constraints in one hold, e.g., covering the mouth
while turning left). A session opens with a two-second Hold Still calibration, followed by a
randomized sequence of active actions, each bounded by a timeout; the validator reports
success or failure for each action.
Decision logic. The deployed system requires every challenge to be completed in time, fuses
calibrated passive scores with per-category weights, and flags a session irrecoverably on
a single high-confidence artifact detection. These engineering choices are not the subject
of this paper: the analysis in Section 5 bypasses the fusion and works on raw per-gesture
detector responses, so the reported ranking is independent of any weighting.

4
Experimental Methodology
Setup and participants. All sessions ran on a single server (NVIDIA RTX 5080, AMD
Ryzen 9, 64 GB RAM), with webcam capture pinned to 1280×720 at 30 FPS via an exact
getUserMedia constraint. Recording took place in three rooms under diffuse daylight,
subjects seated 50–70 cm from the camera. Six volunteers participated under written
consent: three men (25, 27, 51) and three women (24, 24, 49), all from the same broad ethnic
group, appearing in the data only under anonymized identifiers. Each subject recorded 6
genuine and 12 attack sessions, for a total of 108 sessions (36 genuine, 36 DeepFaceLive,
36 FaceFusion), with roles shuffled to suppress learning effects. All decision thresholds
were calibrated before the campaign on recordings of the author only. Each retained session
begins with a two-second Hold Still calibration followed by 5, 6, or 9 active actions (22, 77,
and 9 sessions, respectively; median six). Across the campaign, these actions span pose,
expression, occlusion, and combined or sequential challenges, with their order randomized
within each session. Labels are assigned at recording time.
Attack generation. Attack sessions inject a real-time face swap through a virtual camera.
DeepFaceLive runs pre-trained community .dfm models, two identities per subject with
three sessions each. FaceFusion runs inswapper_128 with the xseg_1 occlusion module
and four reference photos per identity (two frontal, two slight profiles). Attack identities are
matched to the subject on gender and ethnic group, the two attributes that most reliably break
a swap when mismatched, with an ArcFace cosine ranking as a tie-breaker; the campaign
covers 24 attack identities in total.
Analysis protocol. The action window, comprising the frames for which one requested
action is held, is the gesture-level unit of analysis. Gesture and session AUCs use the
mean raw UCF spatial score and bypass the deployed fusion weights and calibrations. We
report ROC AUC between attack and genuine windows by gesture category and generator.
Confidence intervals use 2 000 subject-clustered bootstrap resamples, with comparisons
against the Hold Still baseline paired within each resample. Because the study contains only
six subject clusters and examines several categories–generator contrasts, these intervals are
descriptive and unadjusted for multiplicity. Session scores compare the mean UCF response
from the single initial calibration segment with the mean across all subsequent active-gesture
windows, thereby evaluating active capture as a complete protocol. Challenge-response
latency is the interval from the end of one action’s hold window to the beginning of the
next, converted through the measured session frame rate. Attack sessions include synthesis
and virtual-camera transport that are absent from genuine sessions, so latency reflects
the complete attack condition rather than an isolated human response time. Timed-out
sessions were excluded during collection, but their number and durations were not retained;
we therefore do not treat the observed latency difference as a quantified lower bound.
We additionally report APCER and BPCER for the deployed system following ISO/IEC
30107-3 [In23], while noting that virtual-camera injection falls outside the standard’s
presentation-attack scope.

Scope and limitations.
This proof-of-concept study includes six subjects from one broad
ethnic group and two real-time face-swap tools. Accordingly, the reported performance
and gesture-level effects should be interpreted within the tested population and attack
configurations. The latency results capture the end-to-end overhead of the tested attack
pipelines and may vary across implementations and hardware.
5
Results
5.1
Contribution of the Interactive Layer
Among the 108 retained completed sessions, the deployed system, including fusion and
gesture-completion logic, achieves 94.4 % accuracy, with an APCER of 2.8 % and a BPCER
of 11.1 % [In23]. The observed BPCER indicates that reducing genuine-user rejection
remains an important requirement for practical deployment. To compare the aggregate
active-capture protocol with its initial calibration segment, we use raw UCF scores from
the two segments of the same sessions. The Hold Still calibration segment separates attack
from genuine sessions with an AUC of 0.878 (95 % CI [0.819, 0.963]). The mean across the
active-gesture windows reaches 0.988 [0.966, 1.000], a paired difference of +0.110 [+0.032,
+0.161]. Restricting each session to its first 5 active actions yields a similar AUC of 0.987
[0.968, 1.000]. Across six leave-one-subject-out analyses, the active-window AUC ranges
from 0.983 to 0.998. Figure 2 shows that several attacks overlap the genuine cluster during
calibration but shift upward during the active segment. Thus, under the tested conditions,
aggregating responses across randomized actions improves separation without retraining
the detector.

6
Conclusions
The static calibration segment reaches a session-level AUC of 0.878 against two unseen
attack tools, while the mean across the active-gesture windows reaches 0.988 without
retraining the UCF detector. The category results are less definitive: pose and combined
tasks have the highest point estimates, expressions show no clear improvement, and face
covering against FaceFusion scores below the static baseline. Verifier-observed time-to-
validation provides additional separation, reaching a session-level AUC of 0.732, although
this estimate includes synthesis and transport delay.
Remote verifiers should combine passive inspection with short, randomized actions and
re-evaluate these actions as attack tools change. Response time may add evidence, but

requires deployment-specific calibration. Within its six-subject, two-tool scope, this proof-
of-concept supports active capture as a design principle, not universal performance values
or a fixed gesture ranking.
