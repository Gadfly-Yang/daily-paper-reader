Abstract
Explainable face forgery detection requires not
only correct judgments but also forensic ev-
idence supported by the input image. How-
ever, training cues are typically derived from
the forgery generation pipeline. Since images
available to the model are of limited quality
and resolution, it is difficult to observe the fine-
grained traces these cues refer to. Training
methods that further supply such cues as priv-
ileged supervision exacerbate the problem, as
the student is taught to articulate evidence it
cannot perceive. The student consequently re-
sorts to templated or over-generalized expres-
sions, giving rise to the correct-answer, wrong-
evidence (CAWE) failure. We propose a dual
supportability audit that filters privileged cues
before distillation. Each candidate cue must
be predictable from the remaining privileged
context and causally tied to high-attribution vi-
sual regions in the current image. Only cues
satisfying both conditions receive substantial
distillation weight, preventing unsupported de-
tails from leaking into the student. Experiments
across eight benchmarks show that our method
maintains or improves detection accuracy while
significantly raising explanation faithfulness
compared to existing paradigms.

Introduction
The rapid proliferation of face forgery techniques
poses serious threats to privacy, public trust, and
information security, making reliable face forgery
detection an urgent necessity (Tolosana et al., 2020;
Mirsky and Lee, 2021). Face forgery detection is
now moving beyond binary classification (Rossler
et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2024) toward explain-
able paradigms that produce natural language ev-
idence (Foteinopoulou et al., 2024; Huang et al.,
2024; Sun et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al.,
2024). In these settings, a correct real/fake label
alone is insufficient; the model must also cite visual
anomalies present in the image. Yet a correct an-
swer does not guarantee correct evidence. Recent

We argue that the root cause lies in the supervi-
sion itself. Existing FFD datasets (Rossler et al.,
2019; He et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2024; Yan et al.,
2024) derive their annotations from the forgery gen-

manipulation regions, and modification masks all
describe how a sample was manufactured. Yet the
images available to the model are often of lim-
ited quality and resolution, so many manufacturing
traces are no longer discernible in the final pixels.
Consider a sample annotated as having undergone
face swapping in the lower face: the explanation
accordingly states “jawline blending artifact”, yet
the actual image may exhibit no visible jawline
discontinuity at the given resolution. When such
annotations are used as training targets, the model
is taught to articulate evidence it cannot perceive,
and the supervision reflects how the forgery was
made rather than what can be observed.

align explanations with visual content. Supervised
fine-tuning with label-conditioned rationales (Sun
et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024) degenerates into
memorizing label-to-explanation templates, since

mechanism checks whether each cue is supportable
from the image alone, as shown in Figs. 1 and 4.
We address this issue by introducing two veri-
fication steps before distilling any cue to the stu-
dent. The first step masks the target cue from the
privileged annotation and checks whether the sur-
rounding context alone can still make that cue pre-
dictable. A cue that becomes unpredictable once
masked was copied verbatim from the annotation
and carries no information the student could learn
from. The second step perturbs the image regions
most relevant to the cue and measures whether the
cue likelihood drops accordingly. A cue whose like-
lihood remains unchanged under such perturbation
is not grounded in the current image but instead
reflects dataset-level patterns. Only cues that pass
both checks receive substantial weight during dis-
tillation. This filtering concentrates learning on
evidence the student can genuinely perceive from
the image alone. Our contributions are as follows.
• We trace the correct-answer, wrong-evidence
failure to the gap between pipeline annota-
tions and observability, and show that existing
training strategies do not close this gap.
• We propose a dual supportability audit that
verifies, for each cue, whether it is predictable
from context and sensitive to image content,
and distill only cues that pass both checks.
• Experiments across eight benchmarks show
that our method maintains or improves detec-
tion accuracy while substantially improving
explanation faithfulness.

Related Work
Face Forgery Detection and Explanation. Early
face forgery detection focuses on binary classifica-
tion by exploiting spatial artifacts (Li et al., 2020a;
Shiohara and Yamasaki, 2022), frequency-domain
inconsistencies (Qian et al., 2020), or identity-
aware cues (Dong et al., 2023). Cross-dataset gen-
eralization has been studied through large-scale
benchmarks (Rossler et al., 2019; He et al., 2021;
Yan et al., 2024, 2023b) that expose distribution
shifts across forgery methods.
Advances make detection explainable. Foren-
sic VQA (Foteinopoulou et al., 2024), multi-
modal report generation (Huang et al., 2024),
and vision-language reasoning (Liu et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2024) require articulating manipula-
tion evidence; VLFFD (Sun et al., 2025) pairs de-
tection with visual-linguistic descriptions; Kuck-
reja et al. (Kuckreja et al., 2026) identify the
correct-answer, wrong-evidence failure; PATE-
Forensics (Li et al., 2026) leverages perception

tools for grounded forensic reasoning. However,
explanation supervision derives from manipulation
labels, masks, or forgery categories, none of which
guarantee instance-level visual observability.
Evidence Faithfulness in Multimodal Models.
Producing natural-language evidence does not guar-
antee that it is faithful to visual content. Visual evi-
dence prompting (Praharaj et al., 2025; Zhu et al.;
Guo et al., 2026) injects structured forensic cues
into vision-language models to spatially ground ex-
planations in image regions. RL-based preference
methods (Li et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025b; Jiang
et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026b; Han et al., 2026) ap-
ply GRPO or DPO to reward explanations aligned
with manipulation traces, improving both detection
accuracy and rationale quality. Evidence-assisted
generation (Xu et al., 2026c) constructs chosen-
rejected explanation pairs and optimizes the model
to prefer complete, artifact-grounded evidence over
hallucinated or omitted details. These approaches
refine or reward evidence after the supervision sig-
nal is formed. None examine whether each privi-
leged cue is supportable by the image alone before
it enters the training objective.
Post-training and Privileged Distillation. Or-
thogonal to what evidence to trust, the choice of
training paradigm shapes explanation quality. Su-
pervised fine-tuning with rationales (Sun et al.,
2025; Huang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2022) trains
models to produce chain-of-thought or forensic
explanations conditioned on ground-truth labels,
but label visibility during training encourages tem-
plate memorization rather than visual grounding.
Policy optimization methods such as PPO (Schul-
man et al., 2017), DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), and
GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) instead learn from re-
ward or preference signals. In forensic settings (Li
et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025b), however, these
rewards operate at the response level and do not
verify whether cues are visually supported, leaving
room for plausible but unfaithful evidence. Privi-
leged knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015;
Vapnik and Izmailov, 2015) lets a teacher with priv-
ileged information guide a student with restricted
inputs, and on-policy distillation (Agarwal et al.,
2024) trains the student on its own generations un-
der teacher feedback. Concurrent work audits tool
outputs (Anonymous, 2026b), verifies teacher fixes
by answer recovery (Anonymous, 2026a), or can-
cels generic edit effects with matched controls at
decoding time (Anonymous, 2026c), all to improve
reasoning correctness. Yet none of these methods
test whether each distilled cue is supportable under
the student’s image-only condition. A dual audit is
needed: a context check against cues copied from
the annotation and a visual sensitivity check against
cues ungrounded in the image.
3
Method
Existing explainable FFD methods distill privileged
cues indiscriminately, yet many are unsupported
because annotations describe how a forgery was


manufactured rather than what remains visible. We
propose a dual-audit framework that scores each
candidate cue for contextual predictability and vi-
sual sensitivity before distillation (Fig. 3).
3.1
Cue Supportability Criterion
Task definition.
We define the input as an image
I paired with a task prompt q. The structured an-
notation is g = (y, o, r, c), where y is the real/fake
answer, o is the forgery operation type, r is the
manipulation region, and c is the forensic cue. At
inference, the student sees only I and q; the teacher
also sees o, r, c, masks, or source images.
Two failure modes.
A privileged cue should not
be distilled when it exhibits either (i) direct-cue
dependence: the cue is predictable only when its
own text is visible in the input, indicating mere
copying; or (ii) image-independent prior: the cue
likelihood remains unchanged regardless of which
image is presented, indicating reliance on annota-
tion frequency rather than visual content.

A cue c is
deemed supportable if and only if it exhibits low

Experiments
Datasets.
Across the main, paradigm, and ex-
tended comparisons, we evaluate on eight unique
face forgery detection benchmarks spanning di-
verse forgery types and difficulty levels. For the
main comparison (Table 1), we use four widely
adopted datasets: FaceForensics++ (Rossler et al.,
2019), which contains 1,000 original videos ma-
nipulated by four methods; Celeb-DF v2 (Li et al.,
2020b), featuring high-quality celebrity face swaps;
DFDC (Dolhansky et al., 2020), a large-scale
challenge dataset with diverse perturbations; and
WildDF (Zi et al., 2020), which collects in-the-
wild forgeries from online sources. The training
paradigm comparison (Table 2) reuses Celeb-DF v2
and additionally includes DeeperForensics (Jiang
et al., 2020), ForgeryNet (He et al., 2021), and
Celeb-DF++ (Li et al.); DF40 (Yan et al., 2024) is
evaluated in Appendix G.

Evaluation metrics.
We report four metrics.
ACC and AUC measure detection performance.
EER, the Equal Error Rate, captures the operating
point where false acceptance equals false rejection.
CAWE, the Correct-Answer Wrong-Evidence rate,
quantifies the proportion of correctly classified sam-
ples whose forensic evidence is unsupported by the
input image. For the paradigm comparison, we
additionally report Tok., the student-side training
tokens, and Inf., the average inference tokens per
sample, to assess computational efficiency.

of methods.

4.1
Quantitative Results
Main comparison.
As shown in Table 1, our
method achieves the best or second-best detec-
tion performance across all four benchmarks in
ACC, AUC, and EER, on par with the strongest
explainable baselines EDD and PATE-Forensics.
The key advantage lies in explanation faithfulness:
our CAWE is reduced by 28 to 41% relative to the
strongest competing method across all four bench-
marks, showing that the audit removes visually un-
supported cues without sacrificing accuracy. Tradi-
tional detectors reach competitive AUC but cannot
produce forensic evidence.
Training paradigm comparison.
Table 2 com-
pares our method against 13 training paradigms
across four benchmarks. Our method achieves the
highest ACC on every dataset while attaining the
lowest CAWE. RL methods such as GRPO, DAPO,
and Dr.GRPO improve accuracy over SFT but con-
sume 9 to 12× more training tokens than ours and
produce substantially longer responses, yet their
CAWE remains high, ranging from 67 to 78%. Our
method achieves the strongest trade-off between ac-
curacy and faithfulness with fewer student-side to-
kens than all other distillation and RL alternatives,
and generates the shortest inference responses, as
down-weighting unsupported cues during training
reduces computational cost and output verbosity.

Qualitative results
Fig. 5 compares evidence on two forged sam-
ples. M2F2-Det and Identity-Aware VLM mis-
classify the image as real, while FakeShield and
VLFFD produce correct labels but resort to generic,
template-like evidence that could apply to any
forgery. Our method generates specific cues, such
as the eyeglass-eye boundary inconsistency in the
first sample and the skin-beard transition artifact
in the second, both verifiable in the input image,
while baseline cues stay vague and unlocalized.
4.3
Ablation Study
Fig. 7 reports six ablation groups on FF++, Celeb-
DF v2, and DFDC (FF++ summary in Fig. 1).
Removing TU, VS, or the evidence-first format
(App. B) raises CAWE, and dropping both au-
dits degrades the method to standard OPD with
nearly tripled unfaithfulness, confirming both au-
dits are indispensable (a). The same backbone
and data trained with SFT, DPO, GRPO, or OPD
yield scores above 58%, so the gain stems from
the audit rather than backbone or data (b). Among
distillation targets, cue-masked distillation outper-
forms full-context, base-only, random-drop, and no-
distillation variants, as it blocks literal cue leakage
while keeping privileged guidance (c). Zero-out
masking is the best perturbation, since noise and
replace retain residual information and blur is too
mild (d). The attribution scope k=32 strikes the
best balance: smaller k under-perturbs and larger k
corrupts irrelevant content (e). The full model also
attains the highest ACC among all variants (f).
5
Conclusion
We trace the CAWE failure in forgery detection
to pipeline-derived annotations, which introduce
privileged cues unsupported by the image alone,
and propose a dual supportability audit combining
transfer utility and visual sensitivity that retains
only visually supportable cues. Experiments across
eight benchmarks show that our method substan-
tially reduces CAWE while maintaining detection
accuracy, and a human study confirms that the sup-
portability score tracks human-judged support.


6
Limitations
The audit evaluates cues independently and can
only filter teacher-provided candidates, so it may
miss interacting or unannotated evidence. It also
increases training cost and remains untested be-
yond facial forgeries and single-turn English ex-
planations; broader modalities and settings require
further study. The supportability scores depend
on attribution quality and fixed hyperparameters,
which may require recalibration across architec-
tures or image resolutions.

CAWE Evaluation Protocol
We measure explanation faithfulness with the
Correct-Answer Wrong-Evidence (CAWE) metric,
following the protocol of Kuckreja et al. (Kuckreja
et al., 2026).
Evidence evaluator.
We fine-tune a VLM eval-
uator on image-cue pairs labeled supported or un-
supported. A cue is unsupported if it fails any of
three dimensions:
1. Factual correctness: the artifact is absent from
the image.
2. Visual verifiability:
the artifact cannot be
verified at the given resolution (e.g., “subtle
frequency-domain inconsistency”).
3. Spatial specificity: the cue is too generic to
localize (e.g., “the image looks unnatural”).
Negative pairs pair an image with a cue from an-
other sample, a cue describing invisible artifacts,
or an overly generic cue, violating dimensions 1, 2,
and 3, respectively.
CAWE computation.
Generated explanations
are segmented into cues at the sentence level, each
sentence stating one evidence claim. Among Ncorr
correctly classified samples, each generated cue
is judged independently, and a sample counts as
a CAWE case if any cue is unsupported. With
Nunsup such samples,

E.2
Teacher Robustness
To test whether the high CAWE of Standard OPD
merely reflects limited teacher capacity, we fix
the student (Qwen3-VL-8B) and vary the teacher
across six models from three families (3B–38B) on
FF++.
As shown in Table 9, scaling the teacher within
a family reduces CAWE by only 2–4 points; even
InternVL3-38B retains 54.1% CAWE, and 7B/8B-
class teachers across families stay within 58–60%.
Ours with the default 8B teacher reaches 99.12%
ACC and 21.6% CAWE, over 32 points lower than
any Standard OPD setting. The bottleneck is thus
unsupported privileged cues, not teacher capacity.

Extended Limitations and Future Work
Audit granularity and teacher dependence.
Cues are scored independently, which may under-
estimate jointly supportable combinations (e.g., a
blending boundary visible only with a color shift);
group-level audits or cue graphs could help. The
framework also only filters cues, so artifacts omit-
ted by the teacher annotation cannot be learned;
retrieval-augmented or open-vocabulary cue pro-
posal could complement it.
Domain and deployment scope.
We evaluate
only face forgeries; non-facial deepfakes (e.g.,
