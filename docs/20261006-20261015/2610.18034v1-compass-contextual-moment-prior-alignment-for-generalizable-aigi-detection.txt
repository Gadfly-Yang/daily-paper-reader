ABSTRACT 

Generalizable AI-generated image (AIGI) and deepfake detection remains chal-lenging due to the asymmetry phenomenon , where adaptation to limited train-ing distributions can degrade the transferable representations of Vision Foun-dation Models (VFMs). Recent subspace adaptation methods mitigate this is-sue by freezing high-magnitude principal components while adapting the resid-ual subspace. However, such magnitude-based decomposition is data-agnostic and does not explicitly account for target-relevant semantics, potentially leading to semantic misalignment . We propose COMPASS (COntextual Moment Prior Alignment for Subspace Separation), a data-aware subspace adaptation frame-work that uses a small unlabeled Semantic Proxy Set to estimate target activation statistics. COMPASS incorporates activation covariance into the decomposition of pre-trained weights, preserving target-relevant representations as a stable se-mantic reference while the residual adapter learns forgery-related variations. The proxy calibration requires no gradient-based optimization. Extensive experiments demonstrate strong generalization across cross-dataset, cross-method, joint cross-dataset-method, and open-world cross-generator evaluations, achieving state-of-the-art performance on both Deepfake and AIGI detection benchmarks. Source code and trained models will be publicly released. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in generative AI (Goodfellow et al., 2020; Ho et al., 2020; Rombach et al., 2022a; Yan et al., 2025c; Chen et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2025) enable hyper-realistic synthesis, posing security risks such as deepfakes and visual disinformation (Korshunov & Marcel, 2018; Thies et al., 2016; Shoaib et al., 2023; Singh & Dhumane, 2025; Easttom, 2025). Consequently, robust AI-generated image (AIGI) detection is critical (Wang et al., 2020; Rossler et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2025b; Sarkar et al., 2025; Rathore et al., 2021; Fridrich et al., 2003; Kashyap et al., 2017). A key challenge is the asymmetry phenomenon : detectors overfit to limited forgery patterns and biases (Yan et al., 2025b), yielding entangled features that can hinder generalization to unseen manipulations. To address this issue, recent works explore low-rank fine-tuning of Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) (Radford et al., 2021) via Orthogonal Subspace Decomposition (Wall et al., 2003). EF-FORT (Yan et al., 2025b), a state-of-the-art SVD-based AIGI detector, emphasizes preserving pre-trained VFM knowledge for generalization. It applies SVD to decompose the pre-trained weights into principal and residual components, freezing principal components with the largest magnitudes to retain core semantics, while fine-tuning residual components to capture forgery-specific patterns. The motivation for preserving pre-trained semantics is not to use semantic content itself as a cue for real/fake classification. Rather, real and generated images typically share the same dominant seman-tic content, while forgery-specific signals appear as variations within this shared context. However, its magnitude-based implementation has an important limitation: it prioritizes compo-nents according to their contribution to global weight reconstruction. Although mathematically well-founded, this criterion is data-agnostic and does not explicitly account for task-specific seman-tics. In AIGI detection, substantial distribution shifts arise across dataset domains (e.g., faces or human bodies), manipulation types, and generative models. As a result, preserving high-magnitude components does not guarantee retention of information most relevant for robust forgery detection. Our pilot analysis in fig. 1 illustrates this limitation. Using feature retention, a metric adopted by prior orthogonal adaptation methods (Yan et al., 2025b), we find that magnitude-based decompo-sition can substantially alter the pre-trained representation retained by the principal subspace. We refer to this mismatch between magnitude-based preservation and target-relevant information as se-mantic misalignment . The results further suggest that incorporating target-distribution activation statistics can mitigate this mismatch and improve the preservation of pre-trained representations. Building on this observation, we introduce COMPASS (COntextual Moment Prior Alignment for Subspace Separation), a data-aware subspace adaptation framework that incorporates proxy activa-tion statistics into subspace partitioning. Before fine-tuning, we sample a small unlabeled Semantic Proxy Set (e.g., ∼80 images) from the target-category distribution and compute its activation covari-ance to guide the SVD of pre-trained weights. This lightweight, zero-backpropagation calibration biases the preserved subspace toward directions emphasized by the proxy activation distribution. The proxy samples are used only to estimate activation statistics for subspace calibration and do not participate in gradient-based optimization. Notably, this proxy-guided calibration better preserves pre-trained representations than magnitude-based decomposition. Even when the Semantic Proxy Set is constructed from only one or two datasets within the target category, its activation statistics provide a useful estimate of target-relevant activation structure that remains effective across different data sources. By retaining these target-relevant pre-trained semantics more faithfully, COMPASS establishes a more stable reference for residual fine-tuning, allowing forgery-related variations to be learned with less interference from source-specific content biases. This improved preservation is consistent with the stronger general-ization observed across dataset domains, manipulation types, and generative models. 2We evaluate COMPASS under three challenging generalization settings tables 1 to 3, where it achieves state-of-the-art performance across (1) cross-dataset scenarios, (2) cross-method scenar-ios, (3) joint cross-dataset-method scenarios. The main contributions of this work are: • We identify an important limitation of magnitude-based orthogonal subspace decomposition for AIGI detection: its data-agnostic partitioning may fail to preserve task-relevant pre-trained in-formation. Our pilot analyses show that incorporating proxy-guided calibration can mitigate this 

semantic misalignment and improve feature retention. • We introduce COMPASS (COntextual Moment Prior Alignment for Subspace Separation), which leverages a small unlabeled Semantic Proxy Set to compute activation covariances and uses acti-vation covariance to calibrate subspace preservation before fine-tuning. This lightweight, zero-backpropagation design enables data-aware preservation of pre-trained semantic representations. • We demonstrate that COMPASS achieves state-of-the-art generalization across cross-dataset, cross-method, and joint cross-dataset-method evaluations. Our results support the use of a better-preserved semantic reference for residual forgery learning and show consistent improvements across diverse domains, manipulation types, and generative models. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Conventional Approaches for AIGI Detection. Conventional deepfake and synthetic-image detec-tors primarily exploit task-specific artifacts, including frequency and spatial cues (Li et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2024b), blending inconsistencies (Shiohara & Yamasaki, 2022; Chen et al., 2022a), reconstruction signals (Cao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2024), and content or identity representations (Yan et al., 2023a; Huang et al., 2023). Recent methods further improve artifact-oriented modeling (Liang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025). Despite strong performance, such task-specific cues may generalize poorly to unseen synthesis processes, motivat-ing increasing use of Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) for more transferable forgery detection. 

Vision Foundation Models and Subspace Adaptation. Recent methods leverage VFMs such as CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) to improve generalization in forgery detection (Ojha et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). However, direct fine-tuning may alter pre-trained representations and reduce cross-domain generalization. EFFORT (Yan et al., 2025b) addresses this issue by freezing high-magnitude SVD components while adapting the residual subspace. Its magnitude-based de-composition, however, remains data-agnostic. COMPASS instead uses target activation statistics to guide subspace preservation. 

3 METHOD 

We present COMPASS (CO ntextual Moment Prior Alignment for Subspace Separation), a data-aware subspace adaptation framework for generalizable AIGI detection. As illustrated in fig. 2, COMPASS uses a small unlabeled Semantic Proxy Set to estimate target activation statistics, which guide the decomposition of pre-trained weights into principal and residual weight components. To empirically evaluate COMPASS, we conduct extensive experiments across diverse generalization settings. We first benchmark facial Deepfake detection against 16 methods under cross-dataset, cross-method, and joint cross-dataset-method protocols. We then extend the evaluation to open-world AIGI detection, comparing against 14 competitive detectors across 25 generative models. Finally, we perform a series of ablation and robustness studies to examine the calibration mechanism. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUPS 

Implementation Details. Following EFFORT (Yan et al., 2025b), we use CLIP ViT-L/14 (Radford et al., 2021) as the VFM backbone. The unlabeled Semantic Proxy Set is sampled exclusively from the target-category training split and is used only to estimate activation statistics. No test samples are used for proxy-set, thereby avoiding test-set leakage despite the absence of label supervision. We use Np = 80 for Deepfake detection and Np = 1500 for AIGI detection unless otherwise specified. During fine-tuning, only the residual adapter ∆W is optimized. We use Adam with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −4 and batch sizes of 32 and 48 for Deepfake and AIGI detection, respectively. Following EFFORT, the residual rank is set to 1. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch and trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

Following the evaluation organization of EFFORT (Yan et al., 2025b), we consider two established generalization settings: cross-dataset evaluation based on DeepfakeBench (Yan et al., 2023b) and cross-method evaluation based on DF40 (Yan et al., 2024c). We further introduce a joint cross-dataset-method setting that shifts both the data domain and manipulation method. We compare against 16 representative detectors; detailed baselines and datasets are summarized in tables 1 and 2. 

Evaluation Protocols. 

• Protocol-1 (Cross-dataset): Models trained on FF++ are evaluated on unseen facial datasets while keeping the forgery setting relatively consistent, measuring robustness to domain shift. • Protocol-2 (Cross-method): Models are evaluated on unseen manipulation methods from DF40 (Yan et al., 2024c) while keeping the underlying FF++ data domain fixed, isolating the effect of forgery-method shift. We report Face Swapping as the representative category in the main table. • Protocol-3 (Joint Cross-dataset-method): Both the data domain and manipulation method are shifted simultaneously. Models trained on FF++ are evaluated on Face Reenactment forgeries generated from the CDF-v1 domain in DF40 (Yan et al., 2024c), providing a more challenging double-shift setting. 

Results and Analysis. As shown in table 1, COMPASS achieves comparable or improved per-formance under both single-shift protocols. In cross-dataset evaluation, the gain over EFFORT is 6marginal (0.921 vs. 0.920 Avg. AUC). This setting changes the facial data domain while retain-ing relatively familiar manipulation characteristics, allowing both methods to continue exploiting forgery cues learned from the source distribution. In contrast, the advantage of COMPASS becomes more evident under cross-method evaluation, where the manipulation mechanism itself changes. Although the underlying FF++ domain remains familiar, preserving target-relevant pre-trained rep-resentations becomes more useful when previously learned forgery cues transfer less directly. The advantage is largest under the joint cross-dataset-method setting in table 2, where both the image domain and manipulation method are unseen. COMPASS improves the Avg. AUC from 0.710 for EFFORT to 0.829, an absolute gain of 11.9%. These results suggest that the limitation of data-agnostic, magnitude-based preservation becomes increasingly consequential as fewer source-aligned cues remain available, explaining why the improvement is modest under cross-dataset evaluation but substantially larger under cross-method and joint shifts. For open-world AIGI detection, we follow Setting-II of AIGIBench (Li et al., 2025b). The setting uses 144K training images comprising real images and synthetic images from ProGAN (Karras et al., 2018) and SDv1.4 (Rombach et al., 2022b), and evaluates generalization across 25 generators spanning diverse model families and generation paradigms. We compare COMPASS with 14 representative AIGI detectors, covering CNN-, frequency-, VFM-based, and recent state-of-the-art methods. Detailed baseline information and references are pro-vided in section A.6. As reported in table 3, COMPASS achieves the best average AP of 90.9% across 25 generative mod-els. The improvement is particularly pronounced on several challenging commercial generators. For example, on DALLE-3 (Team, 2024), COMPASS reaches 92.3% AP compared with 60.6% for EF-FORT, an absolute gain of 31.7%. Consistent improvements are also observed on FLUX1-dev (Labs, 2024) and CommunityAI. These results suggest that covariance-guided preservation remains effec-tive under the substantially broader semantic and generative diversity of open-world AIGI detection, where magnitude-based decomposition may preserve less relevant pre-trained information. 4.4 ABLATION AND GENERALIZATION ANALYSIS

5 CONCLUSION 

We present COMPASS, a data-aware subspace adaptation framework for AI-generated image and deepfake detection. Using a small unlabeled Semantic Proxy Set, COMPASS estimates target ac-tivation statistics to guide pre-trained weight decomposition, preserving target-relevant represen-tations as a stable semantic reference while the residual adapter learns forgery-related variations. Experiments across cross-dataset, cross-method, joint cross-dataset-method, and open-world AIGI settings demonstrate strong generalization, with particularly large gains under simultaneous domain and manipulation shifts. Ablations further show robustness to proxy selection, proxy set size, and different VFM backbones. Overall, our results support covariance-guided subspace preservation as an effective strategy for generalizable forgery detection. 9REFERENCES

Conclusion & Empirical Alignment: The systematic scaling and distribution of cosine similarities in fig. 1(b). perfectly align with our mathematical derivation regarding subspace truncation. 

First , the similarity points in COMPASS are more concentrated (Spearman rank correlation ρ =0.5533 ) compared to the broader scatter of EFFORT (Yan et al., 2025b) ( ρ = 0 .1345 ). The higher rank correlation suggests that COMPASS better preserves the relative similarity structure of the original space. By utilizing covariance-guided calibration, COMPASS better preserves the pre-trained representation, perfectly preserving the meaningful numerator (shared semantics ⟨uk, vk⟩)without disrupting structural relationships. 

Second , the upward drift of similarities in COMPASS is more pronounced. Magnitude-based SVD prioritizes components according to their singular values. Its reduction of the vector magnitude (the denominator) is inherently limited, leading to a less-shifted but collapsed representation. In contrast, COMPASS discards components based on semantic irrelevance. It intentionally strips away “magnitude-heavy but semantically useless” domain biases. By discarding a much larger proportion of absolute magnitude (reducing the feature norms more strongly), COMPASS can therefore produce a larger upward shift in cosine similarity after normalization. Architecture & Hardware: Consistent with the baseline EFFORT, we adopt the pre-trained CLIP ViT-L/14 (Radford et al., 2021) as our Vision Foundation Model (VFM) backbone. To accommodate the different scales of the training sets, the deepfake detection models are trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24GB VRAM), while the large-scale AIGI detection models are trained on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (48GB VRAM). 

Optimization & Hyperparameters: During the decoupled forgery feature learning phase, the prior-aligned principal weights Wprin remain strictly frozen to preserve the principal weight com-ponent as a stable semantic reference. We exclusively optimize the low-rank residual adapter. To maintain parameter efficiency and prevent overfitting to domain-specific biases, the residual adapter rank is set to 1. The model is optimized using the Adam optimizer. For the deepfake video detection training phase, the batch size is set to 32 with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −4. For the large-scale open-world AIGI detection task, the batch size is set to 48, with the learning rate adjusted to 1 × 10 −4.

Calibration Phase: The Semantic Proxy Set is constructed using randomly sampled, unlabeled images from the target-category training distribution. The covariance-guided Covariance-Guided SVD is performed entirely offline prior to the training phase, requiring zero backpropagation and introducing no gradient optimization overhead. Training Overhead & Memory Footprint: The proxy-guided subspace calibration step is a highly lightweight, one-time offline process. Extracting features for the minimal proxy set and comput-ing the covariance-guided SVD takes only a few seconds on a single GPU. During the fine-tuning phase, thanks to the low-rank structural bottleneck, COMPASS operates with remarkable resource efficiency: • Deepfake Detection: Operating on the RTX 4090 with a batch size of 32, the peak GPU memory utilization is tightly constrained to approximately 15 GB . One full epoch is pro-cessed rapidly in roughly 16 minutes .• AIGI Detection: Handling the significantly larger AIGIBench on the RTX A6000 with a batch size of 48, the peak GPU memory footprint is maintained at around 22 GB . Despite the massive scale of the data, one full epoch requires only about 87 minutes .

Inference Complexity (FLOPs): Since the trainable adapter ∆W and the frozen principal weights 

Wprin are strictly parallel linear structures, they can be seamlessly re-parameterized into a single weight matrix Wdeploy = Wprin + ∆ W during the deployment phase. Consequently, COMPASS introduces absolutely zero additional inference latency or FLOPs overhead compared to the base zero-shot CLIP model, making it exceptionally well-suited for real-time, real-world generative arti-fact detection scenarios. For completeness, we report the full per-dataset results corresponding to the proxy-source robustness analysis in table 4(a). For each manipulation category, we randomly select two datasets and sample a total of 80 unlabeled images from their training splits to construct the Semantic Proxy Set. The proxy images are used only to estimate activation statistics, and no test images are involved in calibration. We repeat this procedure with different dataset pairs and evaluate the resulting models on all datasets within the same manipulation category. As shown in table 7, COMPASS remains consistently effective across different proxy-source pairs. For Face Swapping, the average AUC ranges from 0.956 to 0.969 across four proxy pairs, compared with 0.939 for EFFORT. For Face Reenactment, the corresponding range is 0.871–0.899 across six proxy pairs, compared with 0.808 for EFFORT. The detailed results support the conclusion in the main paper that COMPASS is relatively insensitive to the particular datasets used to construct a target-relevant proxy set.
